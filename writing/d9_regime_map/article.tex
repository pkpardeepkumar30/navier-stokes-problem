\subtitle{A physical regime map for compressibility, molecular motion,
and unresolved pulse scales}
\author{}
\date{2026-09-12}
\begin{document}
\maketitle


\textbf{A vortex can outgrow the assumptions of an incompressible fluid
model long before its core becomes atomic. The answer depends on how
fast the fluid moves, how rapidly it varies in space, and whether
molecules have time to respond.}

The \href{../d1_core_size/article.html}{first article} connected
increasing speed with decreasing core radius. Its formal extrapolation
reached molecular and atomic lengths. That makes the eventual physical
limitation vivid, but it leaves a practical engineering question: which
assumption needs attention first?

A visible vortex core may contain much finer structures. A gas can
remain slow compared with sound yet vary over distances too short for
the usual continuum description. A liquid requires its own molecular and
thermodynamic assessment. And a computer can accurately evaluate
equations after those equations have ceased to describe the intended
material.

This installment builds a map rather than a single cutoff. We retain the
same illustrative reference---1 mm radius at 1 m/s---and compare air and
water at specified states. We vary the assumed flow-variation length,
compare gas collision and flow times, and audit what the paper actually
tells us about its smaller oscillations. The result identifies useful
next experiments while keeping missing physical information visible.

\subsection{Start with the length over which the flow
changes}\label{start-with-the-length-over-which-the-flow-changes}

Write the core radius as \(a\). The leading scaling gives the anchored
surrogate

\[
a(U)=a_0\left(\frac{U}{U_0}\right)^{-1/(1+2h)}.
\]

The main map uses its inverse-law limit, \(h\to0^+\), so \(aU=a_0U_0\).
The source supplies asymptotic scales; the chosen SI anchor does not
establish a complete viscosity/profile match.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Section 2.1 and Lemma 4.8}

Now introduce a separate length \(L\): the distance over which a
relevant local quantity changes appreciably. For screening, we assume

\[
L=\beta a,\qquad \beta=1,\ 0.1,\ 0.01,\ 0.001.
\]

These are four scenarios. We have not measured \(\beta\) from the
paper's velocity, density, temperature, or phase gradients. A
small-amplitude, short-wavelength pulse also requires its own amplitude
when estimating a velocity gradient; combining the full core speed with
its wavelength can overstate the variation rate.

The distinction matters immediately. Reducing \(L\) by ten increases a
gas Knudsen-number estimate by ten, even though the visible core and its
characteristic speed have not changed.

\subsection{Read the map as a set of
screens}\label{read-the-map-as-a-set-of-screens}

For a sound speed \(c_s\) and gas mean free path \(\lambda_{\rm mfp}\),
use

\[
M=\frac{U}{c_s},\qquad {\rm Kn}_{\rm proxy}=\frac{\lambda_{\rm mfp}}{L}.
\]

We retain the engineering markers \(M=0.3\) and \(\mathrm{Kn}=0.01\).
They indicate where to examine an assumption more carefully; passing
them does not certify a whole flow. Rapid forcing, heating, pressure
changes, and boundary effects can matter independently.
\href{https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19960045736/downloads/19960045736.pdf}{Low-Mach
discussion},
\href{https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20000004526/downloads/20000004526.pdf}{Knudsen-number
discussion}

Air is evaluated at 296.15 K and 101.3 kPa, with
\(\lambda_{\rm mfp}=67.3\) nm under the cited convention. Its sound
speed is an ideal-gas estimate, about 345 m/s. Water is evaluated at
298.15 K and 0.1 MPa, with sound speed about 1497 m/s and estimated mean
molecular spacing 0.311 nm. These properties remain frozen during the
screening calculation.
\href{https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/jres/110/1/j110-1kim.pdf}{NIST
air reference, Equation (11) and Table 3},
\href{https://iapws.org/technical-guidance/release/LiquidWater.download}{IAPWS
water reference, Table 8}

The air Mach marker occurs at about \textbf{103 m/s}, where the assumed
core radius is \textbf{9.66 micrometres}. For water, the corresponding
values are \textbf{449 m/s} and \textbf{2.23 micrometres}.

Those radii are already much larger than atomic lengths. The water
marker is a compressibility comparison only: an absolute-pressure and
phase-change calculation could identify another limitation sooner.

\subsection{Molecules also need time to
respond}\label{molecules-also-need-time-to-respond}

For the air reference, the ideal-gas mean thermal speed is

\[
\bar v=\sqrt{\frac{8RT}{\pi}},\qquad
t_{\rm coll,proxy}=\frac{\lambda_{\rm mfp}}{\bar v}
\approx 1.45\times10^{-10}\ {\rm s}.
\]

This is a convention-dependent collision-time estimate, not a measured
relaxation time for every stress or thermal process. We compare it with
\(t_{\rm flow,proxy}=L/U\):

\[
\chi_{\rm proxy}=\frac{t_{\rm coll,proxy}}{t_{\rm flow,proxy}}
={\rm Kn}_{\rm proxy}\frac{U}{\bar v}.
\]

The last equality explains why these gas screens are related. A small
Knudsen number does not specify the time comparison until a speed is
also supplied. Conversely, \(\chi_{\rm proxy}\) is not an independent
universal failure threshold.

The exported data also include the sound-crossing time \(L/c_s\) and
diffusion time \(L^2/\nu\). Their ratios to \(L/U\) are \(M\) and
\(\mathrm{Re}=UL/\nu\). These are characteristic comparisons. In
particular, \(U/L\) is not the symmetric strain rate: rigid rotation
provides an example with nonzero velocity variation but zero strain.

We leave water's relaxation time unevaluated. Assigning a dilute-gas
collision formula to a dense liquid would conceal the very molecular
physics this audit is meant to examine.

\subsection{What about the paper's much smaller
oscillations?}\label{what-about-the-papers-much-smaller-oscillations}

The source identifies a carrier scale with bare power

\[
\ell_{\rm car}\asymp Q^{(1+h)/2}.
\]

Combining this with the corresponding leading speed scale
\(U\asymp Q^{-1/2-h}\) gives the anchored power-law comparison

\[
\frac{\ell_{\rm car}}{a}
=\frac{\ell_0}{a_0}
\left(\frac{U}{U_0}\right)^{-h/(1+2h)}.
\]

Here we freeze all prefactors to isolate the powers.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
page 81, discussion following Equation (7.22)}

\caption{\label{fig-pulse}The left panel isolates the small change in

\end{figure}%

Using the excluded necessary ceiling \(h=e^{-10}\) gives only about
\textbf{0.078\%} decrease in this normalized ratio between 1 m/s and the
formal endpoint \(0.1c\). The chosen finite \(h=10^{-6}\) is a
sensitivity example, not a certified instance of the paper's full
parameter hierarchy.

This small percentage does \textbf{not} show that the actual pulses are
close to the core size. Their initial ratio is unknown here; local phase
gradients and envelope/cutoff factors remain uncomputed. A physical
phase \(\phi(\mathbf x)\) has local full wavelength
\(2\pi/|\nabla\phi|\), which is not determined by a bare power alone.
The \href{../d5_shearing_wave/article.html}{Kelvin wave from D5}
illustrates shearing and damping, but supplies no missing dimensional
calibration for these pulses.

\subsection{Why ``many molecules'' and ``a local value'' must both
fit}\label{why-many-molecules-and-a-local-value-must-both-fit}

A local density or velocity averages molecular motion over a region
small compared with the flow variation. Let a cubic averaging region
have side \(\epsilon L\), where \(\epsilon<1\). If the mean molecular
spacing is \(d=n^{-1/3}\), its expected particle count is

\[
N=n(\epsilon L)^3=\left(\frac{\epsilon L}{d}\right)^3.
\]

This simple count shows the competing requirements. Taking
\(\epsilon=0.1\) and \(L=10d\) leaves only one molecule on average in
that cube. Taking \(L=100d\) gives 1000. The latter count is an
illustration, not a universal accuracy criterion; correlations and the
observable being averaged matter.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={The number of molecules in a local averaging cube falls rapidly when its size is reduced relative to the flow-variation length.}]{figures/04_averaging_scale_separation.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-average}Expected molecular counts for two
averaging-cube sizes. The cube must be smaller than the scale over which
the flow changes, so counting molecules in the entire vortex is
insufficient. The lines are a geometric calculation, not a molecular
simulation or a universal cutoff.}

\end{figure}%

Water's spacing estimate uses \(d=(M_{\rm mol}/(\rho N_A))^{1/3}\), with
the molar mass from the
\href{https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?Name=water}{NIST Chemistry
WebBook}. The Bohr radius is a separate atomic ruler from
\href{https://physics.nist.gov/cuu/Constants/Table/allascii.txt}{CODATA};
it is not the radius of every atom or a universal fluid cutoff.

The practical choice is conditional. Where the gas remains well
separated from molecular scales but compressibility matters, a
compressible continuum experiment is appropriate. Where variation
lengths approach molecular transport scales, a kinetic description needs
consideration. Liquids require a case-specific constitutive and phase
assessment. Relativistic hydrodynamics does not repair a lost molecular
averaging scale.

The \href{model-selection.md}{model-selection note} defines a tractable
first comparison: a smooth rotating gas vortex in radial balance,
comparing constant-density pressure support with a compressible
ideal-gas equilibrium. Its observable is the density deficit and
pressure difference as the rotation speed increases. Keeping the
velocity profile and exterior state explicit will let that experiment
isolate a physical effect without pretending to continue the proposed
blowup solution.

Eight scientific checks cover independent scale elimination, numerical
threshold roots, Maxwell-speed integration, time-scale identities, and
averaging-volume counts. The \href{source-notes.md}{source notes},
\href{../../code/d9_regime_map/study.ipynb}{executed notebook},
\href{../../code/d9_regime_map/parameters.json}{parameters}, and
\href{../../code/d9_regime_map/data/}{CSV data} record the assumptions
and reproduce the map.
