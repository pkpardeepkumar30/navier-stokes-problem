\subtitle{Reading a Navier--Stokes residual as a balance of
accelerations}
\author{}
\date{2026-09-11}
\begin{document}
\maketitle


\textbf{Prescribing a shrinking, accelerating vortex also prescribes the
force needed to sustain it.} The Navier--Stokes momentum residual tells
us what that force would have to be. A velocity field that looks
plausible, is divergence free, and has finite energy can still require a
force that becomes unbounded.

That is the missing question after the
\href{../d2_finite_energy/article.html}{previous article's energy
calculation}. There we chose a smooth vortex whose peak grows as its
width shrinks. We showed how its energy can decrease. We did not ask
whether the acceleration, pressure, and viscosity actually balance with
an acceptable external force.

This distinction matters in the proposed OpenAI construction. The paper
seeks a velocity that becomes unbounded while the required external
force remains smooth through the singular time. Its leading background
alone leaves a residual that must be treated by additional corrections.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Section 2 and Equation (3.1)}

We will calculate the individual terms for two explicit fields: the
paper's outer heat-flow formula, where the momentum terms cancel, and
our prescribed Gaussian vortex, where they do not. This evaluates a
concrete part of the paper's flow and an educational comparison. The
joined inner core and annulus are not reconstructed in this installment.

\subsection{A residual is a force balance you can
inspect}\label{a-residual-is-a-force-balance-you-can-inspect}

Imagine a one-kilogram cart accelerating at three metres per second
squared. If the forces already accounted for supply only two newtons,
one newton remains to be explained. A residual plays the same role in a
differential equation.

For incompressible Navier--Stokes, write

\[
\mathbf R(\mathbf u,p)=
\underbrace{\partial_t\mathbf u}_{\text{local acceleration}}
+\underbrace{(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u}_{\text{transport}}
-\underbrace{\nu\Delta\mathbf u}_{\text{viscous term}}
+\underbrace{\nabla p}_{\text{pressure term}}.
\]

Pressure here is divided by the constant density. Each term has the
units of acceleration in a dimensional formulation; \(\mathbf R\) is the
external force \textbf{per unit mass} required by the chosen velocity
and pressure.

For a specified force \(\mathbf f\), the momentum equation says
\(\mathbf R=\mathbf f\). When checking that equation, the defect is
\(\mathbf R-\mathbf f\). When no external force is intended, the
residual should vanish.

Incompressibility is a separate condition, \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf u=0\).
Satisfying it does not make the momentum residual vanish.

\subsection{A circular flow still has transport
acceleration}\label{a-circular-flow-still-has-transport-acceleration}

Both examples have only an azimuthal velocity,

\[
\mathbf u=u_\theta(r,z,t)\,\mathbf e_\theta.
\]

The cylindrical components are independent of angle. Nevertheless, a
particle moving around a circle changes its velocity direction. Its
transport acceleration has the radial component

\[
\big[(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u\big]_r
=-\frac{u_\theta^2}{r}.
\]

This is the familiar inward centripetal acceleration. Treating the
cylindrical components as if they were Cartesian scalar fields would
miss it.

We choose pressure to provide the corresponding balance:

\[
\partial_r p=\frac{u_\theta^2}{r},
\qquad
p(r,z,t)=-\int_r^\infty\frac{u_\theta(s,z,t)^2}{s}\,ds.
\]

The radial transport and pressure terms therefore cancel. The other
directions still need checking. In particular, the angular component of
the vector Laplacian is

\[
(\Delta\mathbf u)_\theta
=\partial_{rr}u_\theta+\frac1r\partial_r u_\theta
-\frac{u_\theta}{r^2}+\partial_{zz}u_\theta.
\]

The \(-u_\theta/r^2\) term is another geometric contribution that
matters.

\subsection{Two fields, two different
balances}\label{two-fields-two-different-balances}

\subsubsection{The explicit outer flow from the
paper}\label{the-explicit-outer-flow-from-the-paper}

Equation (4.29) provides an outer swirl that is independent of height
and solves the radial heat equation at viscosity one:

\[
\partial_t u_\theta
=\partial_{rr}u_\theta+\frac1r\partial_r u_\theta-\frac{u_\theta}{r^2}.
\]

For this field, local angular acceleration balances viscosity. Its
pressure is independent of height as well, so there is no axial pressure
force. Together with the radial balance above, these identities give
\textbf{zero momentum residual} on the exterior domain.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Theorem 4.6(v), page 33}

We evaluate that explicit family with normalization \(c_\infty=1\) and
\(h=10^{-6}\). These choices do not assemble a full paper profile: its
matching boundary and other parameter constraints remain uncalibrated.
The formula is used only at positive radius. Continuing it to the axis
would produce a singular field, and its independence of height prevents
finite whole-space energy.

\subsubsection{The smooth Gaussian from the previous
article}\label{the-smooth-gaussian-from-the-previous-article}

Our toy swirl is

\[
u_\theta=U R\exp\left(\frac{1-R^2-Z^2}{2}\right),
\qquad R=\frac r a,\quad Z=\frac z b.
\]

In the \(h=0\) limiting example, \(a=\sqrt{\tau}\), \(b=2\sqrt{\tau}\),
and \(U=\tau^{-1/2}\), with \(\tau=1-t\). Its pressure integral can be
evaluated explicitly:

\[
p=-\frac{U^2}{2}\exp(1-R^2-Z^2).
\]

This pressure cancels the radial acceleration. But the angular time
derivative does not cancel the viscous term, and the pressure varies
with height. There is both an angular and, away from the midplane, an
axial force left over.

\subsection{The required force can grow faster than the
speed}\label{the-required-force-can-grow-faster-than-the-speed}

At a fixed similarity location in the Gaussian example, the speed scales
as \(\tau^{-1/2}\). The angular residual and axial pressure gradient
scale as \(\tau^{-3/2}\). For the marker used here, the pointwise force
magnitude therefore grows by a factor of one thousand whenever the
remaining time decreases by a factor of one hundred.

Integrating the squared force over space gives another useful measure.
The volume scale is \(\tau^{3/2}\), so

\[
\|\mathbf R\|_{L^2}^2
\ \text{scales like}\ \tau^{-3}\tau^{3/2}=\tau^{-3/2},
\qquad
\|\mathbf R\|_{L^2}\ \text{scales like}\ \tau^{-3/4}.
\]

The force norm grows even though this toy's kinetic energy decreases.
Thus its finite-energy concentration does not produce the smooth
external force sought in the paper.

The Gaussian integral is evaluated on \(0<r<8a\), \(|z|<8b\), with
quadrature refinement checks. This captures its rapidly decaying force
field for the reported calculation. The exterior curve is a pointwise
check along shrinking-radius markers; it is not a whole-space energy or
force estimate.

\subsection{Check the arithmetic before interpreting the
cancellation}\label{check-the-arithmetic-before-interpreting-the-cancellation}

We report the absolute residual as well as a normalized value. For each
cylindrical component \(j\), define

\[
\epsilon_j=
\frac{|R_j|}
{|(\partial_t\mathbf u)_j|
+|((\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u)_j|
+|(-\nu\Delta\mathbf u)_j|
+|(\nabla p)_j|}.
\]

A small value indicates strong cancellation relative to the terms in
that component. If every term is zero, we record zero by convention. We
keep the components separate because large radial terms could otherwise
hide an inaccurate angular balance.

The exterior's profile is evaluated by numerical quadrature. Increasing
its order improves the derivative and residual until floating-point
error dominates. We compare selected values with 60-digit integration,
and independently differentiate the velocity and pressure with respect
to physical coordinates. Pressure's radial cancellation is imposed
analytically; the derivative of its separately computed integral
provides a check on that implementation.

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Quadrature errors decrease to a floating-point plateau, while finite-difference errors first improve and then worsen as the step decreases.}]{figures/03_numerical_checks.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-checks}Increasing quadrature order initially
improves the exterior's profile derivative and angular cancellation. In

In this run, the largest normalized angular residual along the exterior
time scan is approximately \textbf{5.6e-15}. A small relative value can
coexist with an increasing absolute numerical remainder when the
individual terms grow. Both measures are exported in the data.

These checks verify a finite computation against known identities. They
do not show that an arbitrary residual and every one of its derivatives
extend smoothly through a singular time.

\subsection{What this adds to the construction
story}\label{what-this-adds-to-the-construction-story}

The residual gives us a concrete way to ask what a prescribed flow
requires. Our Gaussian vortex passes the earlier divergence and energy
checks but needs an unbounded force. The explicit heat exterior
satisfies its local balances, but it supplies only one part of the full
construction.

The paper's next challenge is to join an appropriate inner flow to its
exterior and deal with the remaining imbalance in the connecting region.
Its oscillatory corrections use averaged velocity products to supply the
missing momentum transport. The next installment will explain how a
velocity oscillation with zero mean can still have a nonzero mean
stress.

\subsection{Technical note: the exterior
formula}\label{technical-note-the-exterior-formula}

To avoid confusing the paper's squared-radius variable with the previous
article's time ratio, write \(\sigma=r^2/2\) here. Equation (4.29) is

\[
u_\theta=c_\infty\sigma^{-A}\mathcal H(2\tau/\sigma),
\qquad A=\frac12+h,
\]

\[
\mathcal H(\zeta)=\frac1{\Gamma(1+h)}
\int_0^\infty e^{-v}v^h(1+\zeta v)^{-h}\,dv.
\]

Differentiation under the integral gives the needed derivatives. The
quadrature uses the weight \(e^{-v}v^h\). We evaluate pressure by
changing its radial integral to a finite interval. The source notes give
the derivative formulas and normalization.

The chosen \(h=10^{-6}\) is below the necessary ceiling discussed in D1,
but we do not claim it satisfies the full construction's parameter
hierarchy. The exterior formula can be studied as an explicit family
without asserting that its chosen normalization has been joined to a
completed inner profile.
