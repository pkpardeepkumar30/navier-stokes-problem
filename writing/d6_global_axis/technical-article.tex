\subtitle{D6 stage D: bounding the axis amplitude and checking the inner
exit}
\author{}
\date{2026-09-12}
\begin{document}
\maketitle


\textbf{A locally accurate vortex profile can still fail the conditions
needed to connect it to the exterior. We found such a failure in a
narrow parameter band, then recovered the required signs at the tested
points by increasing the radial scale parameter. We also constructed an
analytic bound that keeps the axis amplitude under control.}

The \href{../d6_axis_pressure/article.html}{previous installment}
computed pressure at the axis from the paper's outer radial schedule.
That supplied a meaningful input to the inner solver. It did not
establish that the resulting inner profile was suitable everywhere along
the axis.

There are two reasons to check further. First, the axis amplitude
depends exponentially on a large parameter, so a normalization that
works at one location can be unsuitable elsewhere. Second, the paper
requires specific shear and source inequalities before the inner field
can be continued outward. Solving the local differential equations does
not automatically enforce those inequalities.

This study addresses both issues. It supplies a conservative analytic
amplitude bound and tests the inner conditions on a broad grid and two
finer grids near a critical axis location. The distinction between a
bound and a numerical test remains important: the amplitude estimate
covers an entire specified complex neighborhood, while the inner exit
results cover the declared sample points.

\subsection{Why a logarithm is the useful
quantity}\label{why-a-logarithm-is-the-useful-quantity}

The normalized axis amplitude is

\[
g(\eta)=\frac{1}{C}\exp\!\left(\Lambda S(\eta)\right),
\qquad
S(\eta)=\int_0^\eta\zeta(w)\,dw.
\]

Here \(\eta\) is the axial similarity parameter, \(\Lambda\) sets the
radial scale, and \(C\) is an amplitude normalization chosen afterward.
The source defines

\[
\zeta=-\frac{LH_*}{H_*^2+\sigma^2},\qquad
H_*=(1/2-h)\eta+(1-\eta^2)(4\eta+j),\qquad
L=1-2h\eta^2.
\]

The small parameter \(h\) controls the collapse scaling, \(j\) offsets
the axial velocity at the axis, and \(\sigma\) smooths the amplitude
variation near a zero of \(H_*\). That zero lies slightly below
\(\eta=0\), and the amplitude can grow rapidly nearby. Storing \(C\) or
the exponential directly as ordinary floating-point numbers is therefore
impractical. We instead store

\[
\log g=\Lambda S-\log C.
\]

The code retains extra arithmetic digits when forming this expression.
It also evaluates the rational integrand's antiderivative through its
complex poles, with an independent split quadrature check. This avoids
missing the narrow variation in the integrand.

\subsection{An amplitude bound over an entire
neighborhood}\label{an-amplitude-bound-over-an-entire-neighborhood}

The paper requires \(C\) to dominate the unnormalized amplitude on a
complex neighborhood of the real interval. This complex extension is a
device for controlling derivatives; it does not introduce a physically
complex-valued fluid.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Equation (B.16), pages 147--148}

We choose a thin rectangle around \([-1,1]\) and bound the integrand
throughout it. Explicit lower bounds on its denominator factors exclude
poles of \(\zeta\) throughout the rectangle. A straight integration path
then bounds \(|S|\), giving a sufficient choice of \(\log C\).

The bound is deliberately conservative. Its arithmetic uses exact
rational numbers formed from the declared decimal inputs, and the
exported value of \(\log C\) is rounded upward. It does not depend on a
grid finding the largest amplitude. The \href{source-notes.md}{source
notes} give the complete inequalities.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={The logarithmic amplitude remains finite, and the chosen complex strip lies between the nearest poles of the rational axis integrand.}]{figures/01_logarithmic_amplitude_bound.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-amplitude}Left: the axis amplitude is represented
through its logarithm. Right: a local view of the complex neighborhood
and its nearest integrand poles, with both coordinates scaled by
sigma/H\_star'. The amplitude bound uses analytic estimates over the
entire rectangle, rather than these plotted pole locations.}

\end{figure}%

For the selected inputs the rectangle's imaginary half-width is about
\textbf{1.47e-06}. The resulting normalization satisfies the source's
amplitude inequality. This single estimate does not establish the other
parameter choices or the complete vortex construction.

\subsection{The failure appears near a shifted critical
location}\label{the-failure-appears-near-a-shifted-critical-location}

The inner profile must have positive angular shear \(p_1\). The source
also gives a lower bound on its angular source \(S_q\). At the outer
edge of the inner region it requires

\[
p_1+\frac{p_2^2}{p_1}>2+c_{\mathrm{ex}},
\]

where \(p_2\) measures the axial contribution. We test the declared
threshold \(2.2\), corresponding to \(c_{\mathrm{ex}}=0.2\).
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{Equations
(B.17)--(B.19), pages 148--150}

A broad grid gives an incomplete picture. Near the zero \(\eta_H\) of
\(H_*\), the coupled pressure and axial flow introduce a still narrower
scale,

\[
\Delta_\eta=\frac{Z_*(\eta_H)}{\Lambda H_*'(\eta_H)}.
\]

Here \(Z_*\) is the paper's axial source expression evaluated at the
axis. We use this scale to place additional points at
\(\eta=\eta_H+c\Delta_\eta\).

To compare radial scale choices against the imposed pressure, we use
\(\Lambda/P_*^2\), where \(P_*^2\) sets the magnitude of the outer
pressure input computed in stage C. The choice \(\Lambda/P_*^2=10^{16}\)
gives negative \(p_1\) at some of the targeted points: these finite
parameters miss a required condition. Increasing radial degree and
precision preserves that failure in the local calculation. At the larger
declared value \(\Lambda/P_*^2=10^{24}\), the sampled signs recover.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={A targeted narrow-band scan reveals sign failures for the smaller radial scale parameter and positive sampled margins for the larger one.}]{figures/02_hidden_parameter_band.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-band}Both panels resolve displacement from the zero
of H\_star in units of the pressure-dependent width for each parameter
choice. The smaller Lambda produces negative shear and a negative source
margin in part of the band. The larger Lambda passes these sampled
signs. The vertical axes use a symmetric logarithmic scale to retain
both signs.}

\end{figure}%

For the larger value, \(\Delta_\eta\) is approximately
\textbf{1.96e-26}. All \textbf{19} coordinates in this finest scan
collapse to \textbf{1} distinct value when converted to binary64. We
keep them as high-precision decimals throughout the calculation.

This is a dimensionless width in the similarity parameter. It is not an
inferred molecular distance. Its computational message is that
increasing radial resolution alone cannot recover information already
lost when storing the axial coordinates.

\subsection{Check the exit without overflowing the axial
term}\label{check-the-exit-without-overflowing-the-axial-term}

Near the zero of \(H_*\), the angular contribution can be very small.
The source's exit argument uses the axial contribution there; away from
that region the angular contribution can suffice.

The axial ratio can be enormous because it contains the inverse of the
tiny amplitude \(g\). We evaluate its logarithm and report a useful
lower bound: where the angular term is used alone, we retain \(p_1\); in
the complementary region, we add \(\min(p_2^2/p_1,3)\). Capping a
nonnegative term this way reduces the exit expression, so passing with
this lower bound is sufficient at that point.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={The angular and axial contributions provide complementary sampled exit margins, while a separate radial-degree scan checks equation consistency and profile convergence.}]{figures/03_sampled_exit_and_convergence.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-exit}Left: the angular contribution and a stated
lower bound on the exit expression across the wider critical region.
Right: radial-degree convergence at a separate declared point. The exit
results are pointwise numerical checks; the amplitude bound above has a
different, analytic scope.}

\end{figure}%

The smallest reported exit lower bound is \textbf{3.000}, exceeding
\(2.2\). The normalized swirl remains positive at all declared profile
samples. Replacing the 128-node pressure input with its 256-node
counterpart changes the selected critical source margin by about
\textbf{7.5e-17}.

These checks support the reported numerical observations. They do not
supply a bound between every pair of sample points or a rigorous
remainder estimate for the inner series.

\subsection{What this adds to D6}\label{what-this-adds-to-d6}

We now have an explicit complex-neighborhood amplitude bound, a
logarithmic implementation, and targeted numerical evidence for the
inner exit conditions. We also have a concrete example of why a locally
converged solution and a coarse parameter scan can be insufficient.

The remaining work includes uniform control of the inner approximation,
the outer construction's parameter and stress conditions, annular
matching, and the first correction in the paper's time-scale expansion.
The
\href{../d6_moment_correction/implementation-ledger.md}{implementation
ledger} tracks those dependencies.

This is still background-profile work. A useful conceptual comparison
for the later oscillatory stage is convex integration: oscillations can
create an averaged momentum flux that cancels a residual stress. That is
our comparison of mechanisms; the paper also relies on dynamical
amplification and viscous decay. D6's present calculations prepare the
background and its corrections before that oscillatory stage.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{Paper,
pages 2--5 and Sections 7--9}
