\subtitle{A controlled comparison between constant-density and
compressible gas equilibria}
\author{}
\date{2026-09-12}
\begin{document}
\maketitle


\textbf{For the smooth vortex examined here, the center density falls
5\% below the surrounding gas density when the peak rotation speed is
about 0.193 times the exterior sound speed. At a speed ratio of 0.3, the
deficit is nearly 12\%.}

The \href{../d9_regime_map/article.html}{physical regime map} explained
why compressibility can matter while a vortex is still comfortably
larger than molecular transport scales. A screening number identifies a
question; a model with an equation of state lets us calculate an answer.

Here the question is concrete: how much must the gas density and
pressure change to support a prescribed rotating velocity profile? We
compare a constant-density fluid with a compressible ideal gas, using
the same swirl and the same pressure far from the vortex. We solve the
radial balance numerically and check it against an analytic equilibrium.

This is a steady, inviscid Euler benchmark. Its Gaussian isentropic
vortex belongs to a well-established test family. It provides a
controlled physical comparison, not a simulation of the OpenAI collapse
or evidence that compressibility prevents singularities.
\href{https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20150018403/downloads/20150018403.pdf}{Spiegel,
Huynh and DeBonis, Sections III--IV}

\subsection{Keep the velocity profile
explicit}\label{keep-the-velocity-profile-explicit}

Let \(a\) be the radius of peak speed and write \(R=r/a\). Prescribe

\[
u_\theta(r)=U R\exp\!\left(\frac{1-R^2}{2}\right),\qquad u_r=u_z=0.
\]

The velocity vanishes smoothly at the axis, peaks at \(r=a\), and decays
at large radius. We use a fixed \(a=1\) mm. Unlike the earlier collapse
estimate, the radius does not decrease when we increase \(U\): each
speed defines a separate equilibrium.

The exterior air state is 296.15 K and 101.3 kPa. We take the ideal-gas
constants \(\gamma=1.4\) and \(R_{\rm gas}=287.05\) J/(kg K), giving
exterior density \(\rho_\infty=p_\infty/(R_{\rm gas}T_\infty)\) and
sound speed \(c_\infty=\sqrt{\gamma R_{\rm gas}T_\infty}\).

We label the control parameter

\[
M_*=\frac{U}{c_\infty}.
\]

It uses the \textbf{exterior} sound speed. The local Mach number also
depends on the local temperature and is evaluated separately.

\subsection{Pressure must increase outward to hold the gas in a
circle}\label{pressure-must-increase-outward-to-hold-the-gas-in-a-circle}

Radial momentum balance is

\[
\frac{dp}{dr}=\rho(r)\frac{u_\theta(r)^2}{r}.
\]

Pressure is lower toward the axis, providing the inward force for
circular motion. The constant-density model fixes \(\rho=\rho_\infty\),
so its pressure follows directly by integration from the exterior:

\[
\frac{p_{\rm inc}(R)}{p_\infty}
=1-\frac{\gamma e}{2}M_*^2e^{-R^2}.
\]

For the compressible gas, pressure and density are linked by the
constant-entropy relation \(p/\rho^\gamma=p_\infty/\rho_\infty^\gamma\).
With the ideal-gas equation of state, the specific enthalpy is
\(H=\gamma p/[(\gamma-1)\rho]\). Radial momentum becomes
\(dH/dr=u_\theta^2/r\).
\href{https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/airplane/isentrop.html}{NASA
isentropic relations}

Define

\[
\Theta(R)=1-\frac{(\gamma-1)e}{2}M_*^2e^{-R^2}.
\]

The resulting gas equilibrium is

\[
\frac{T}{T_\infty}=\Theta,\qquad
\frac{\rho}{\rho_\infty}=\Theta^{1/(\gamma-1)},\qquad
\frac{p}{p_\infty}=\Theta^{\gamma/(\gamma-1)}.
\]

These fields require \(\Theta>0\). The implementation rejects parameters
giving a nonpositive center temperature.

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Faster swirl creates a deeper density depression. Compressible and constant-density pressure profiles agree at low speed and diverge as speed increases.}]{figures/01_density_and_pressure.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-profiles}The same prescribed rotation produces
different density and pressure profiles depending on the constitutive
model. Both pressure curves share the same exterior value. The dashed
constant-density pressure can become negative after that exterior
absolute pressure is fixed; its interpretation is discussed below.}

\end{figure}%

This is a comparison of equilibria with different interior densities,
not two evolutions of identical initial data. Both models have zero
external force and omit viscosity. Steady mass conservation follows
because the motion is purely azimuthal and the density is axisymmetric.
Energy transport is also azimuthal, with no angular variation, so the
steady total-energy equation is satisfied.

\subsection{A density tolerance gives a better-defined
marker}\label{a-density-tolerance-gives-a-better-defined-marker}

Choose a 5\% center density deficit as an illustrative tolerance:

\[
1-\frac{\rho(0)}{\rho_\infty}=0.05.
\]

Solving gives \(M_*=\) \textbf{0.193}, or about \textbf{66.7 m/s} for
this exterior state. At \(M_*=0.3\), the deficit is \textbf{11.8\%}.

The central gas is stationary on the axis even though its density
deficit is largest there. Pressure support depends on the surrounding
swirl. Thus a single local speed, or a generic Mach marker, does not
uniquely determine the density variation across a vortex.

Expanding at small \(M_*\) gives

\[
1-\frac{\rho(0)}{\rho_\infty}=\frac{e}{2}M_*^2+O(M_*^4),
\qquad
\frac{p(0)-p_{\rm inc}(0)}{p_\infty}
=\frac{\gamma e^2}{8}M_*^4+O(M_*^6).
\]

The coefficient depends on the chosen swirl shape. The 0.193 value is
therefore a result for this profile and tolerance, not a replacement
universal Mach cutoff.

\subsection{What does negative constant-density pressure mean
here?}\label{what-does-negative-constant-density-pressure-mean-here}

The constant-density center pressure reaches zero at \(M_*\approx\)
\textbf{0.725}, after fixing the same exterior absolute pressure. At
\(M_*=0.8\) it is negative, while the compressible gas still has
positive pressure, density, and temperature.

An incompressible pressure field is a mathematical constraint field,
determined up to an additive constant in this setting. Negative values
do not invalidate that mathematical solution. They show that this
particular field, with its exterior absolute pressure fixed, cannot
simply be inserted into an ideal-gas thermodynamic interpretation.

The compressible family has its own positivity limit,
\(M_*<\sqrt{2/[(\gamma-1)e]}\). We do not approach that limit in the
calculation or interpret it as a dynamically reached event.

\subsection{Check the computation before interpreting the
difference}\label{check-the-computation-before-interpreting-the-difference}

We integrate an independent nonlinear radial equation for the normalized
density \(q=\rho/\rho_\infty\):

\[
\frac{dq}{dR}=M_*^2 R e^{1-R^2}q^{2-\gamma}.
\]

A fourth-order Runge--Kutta method starts at an outer radius and
proceeds inward. With the exact outer density, the maximum density error
decreases by approximately a factor of sixteen when the radial step is
halved. The finest displayed error is about \textbf{4.6e-12}, measured
relative to exterior density.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={A refined radial solver converges with exact boundary data, while an approximate far-field boundary leaves an error floor that decreases when the domain is enlarged.}]{figures/03_radial_and_boundary_accuracy.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-accuracy}Radial refinement gives fourth-order
convergence when the correct boundary value is supplied. Fixing the
density to its infinite-radius value at a finite outer radius leaves a
separate boundary error. The right panel separates that contribution
from integration error. Its small dip in total error is cancellation
between signed errors, not a sudden increase in accuracy of the method.}

\end{figure}%

We also check all four steady Cartesian conservation equations---mass,
two momentum components, and total energy---using independent finite
differences of the conservative fluxes. Additional checks cover the
enthalpy integral, equation of state, constant entropy, small-Mach
limits, actual swirl shear, and the density marker.

These are checks of a stationary radial solver and an equilibrium field.
There is no flow time step, two-dimensional time integration, or
dynamical stability result.

\subsection{Is this comparison already at molecular
scales?}\label{is-this-comparison-already-at-molecular-scales}

For the fixed 1 mm radius, the exterior mean-free-path ratio is
\(6.73\times10^{-5}\). We improve on that core-scale comparison by
computing actual thermodynamic gradients:

\[
\mathrm{Kn}_f=\lambda_{\rm mfp}(r)\,|\nabla\log f|,
\qquad f=T,\rho,p.
\]

The local mean-free-path estimate uses the reference NIST
temperature/pressure correction with the calculated gas state. It is a
local-property approximation, not a kinetic simulation. We also compare
its collision-time proxy with the explicitly computed swirl shear
\(|du_\theta/dr-u_\theta/r|\).
\href{https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/jres/110/1/j110-1kim.pdf}{NIST,
Equation (11)}

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Thermodynamic-gradient Knudsen proxies and collision-time/shear ratios remain small for all displayed equilibria.}]{figures/04_local_gas_scale_check.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-gas}For the chosen fixed radius and speed range, the
computed thermodynamic-gradient Knudsen proxy remains well below 0.01.
The collision-time/shear comparison is also small. Zero values on the
axis lie below the logarithmic display. These diagnostics support scale
separation for this benchmark under its property approximations, not all
assumptions of an actual rotating gas.}

\end{figure}%

Across the scan, the largest thermodynamic-gradient Knudsen proxy is
about \textbf{0.00018} and the largest collision/shear ratio is
\textbf{5.9e-05}. Density can therefore vary substantially in this
benchmark while these molecular-scale screens remain small.

Other assumptions still have limits. The largest case reaches a center
temperature near \textbf{193 K}; constant heat capacities and the local
transport estimate are modelling approximations. Viscosity would
redistribute angular momentum and generate heat. The stationary Euler
family does not describe indefinite persistence of a real viscous
vortex.

\subsection{What this contributes to the
project}\label{what-this-contributes-to-the-project}

This experiment replaces a generic compressibility warning with a
defined observable, a consistent equation of state, and a validated
equilibrium calculation. It shows how a physically meaningful density
change can arise well before the selected molecular screens, while
preserving a clear boundary between the equilibrium example and the
proposed blowup construction.

The \href{research-decision.md}{research decision} classifies this as an
educational reconstruction of a known vortex family. A study of the
actual collapsing flow still requires its assembled profiles and
consistent compressible initial data, forcing, boundary conditions, and
energy inputs.
