\subtitle{D6 stage B: a numerical solver for the leading inner
equations}
\author{}
\date{2026-09-12}
\begin{document}
\maketitle


\textbf{We can now compute the paper's leading inner profile equations
for explicit analytic axis data and check how their balance improves
with radial resolution. The chosen data validate the solver; they do not
yet define the paper's joined vortex.}

The \href{../d6_moment_correction/article.html}{finite moment repair}
showed how a small linear solve can cancel five integral discrepancies.
To use that repair in a full correction, however, we need the velocity
and pressure that generate those discrepancies. An isolated exterior
solution or an arbitrary Gaussian vortex does not provide them.

The next step is therefore to construct the inner field itself. The
paper defines its equations near the vortex axis, where the flow must
remain smooth in Cartesian coordinates. Their radial form appears
singular, but regularity supplies a natural way to compute successive
coefficients.

This article explains that calculation, checks it in both similarity and
physical coordinates, and identifies what still prevents a complete
reconstruction. A useful lesson emerges: solving local balance equations
accurately and satisfying all the global construction requirements are
separate tasks.

\subsection{Remove the apparent singularity before
computing}\label{remove-the-apparent-singularity-before-computing}

The source writes the azimuthal profile as

\[
E(X,\eta)=\sqrt{2X}\,F(X,\eta),
\]

where \(X\) is the radial similarity coordinate and \(\eta\) is the
axial similarity parameter. The square-root factor gives the required
linear vanishing of physical swirl at the axis. Smoothness is imposed on
\(F\), the axial velocity profile \(U\), the pressure \(\Pi\), and
\(V_0/X\), where \(V_0\) determines radial velocity.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Equations (4.3)--(4.7), pages 25--26}

We also use the paper's rescaled radial coordinate \(Y=\Lambda X\) and
factor out the axis amplitude:

\[
F(Y,\eta)=g(\eta)\Phi(Y,\eta),\qquad \Phi(0,\eta)=1.
\]

Computing \(\Phi\) avoids confusing variation in the radial profile with
the potentially enormous variation of its axis amplitude. The pressure
increment is retained separately from the axis pressure for a similar
numerical reason.

\subsection{The axis data are inputs to this local
problem}\label{the-axis-data-are-inputs-to-this-local-problem}

The validation example uses

\[
U(0,\eta)=4\eta+0.02,\qquad
\Pi(0,\eta)=-\frac{100}{(1+\eta^2)^2}.
\]

The positive axis amplitude follows the source's functional form:

\[
g(\eta)=C^{-1}\exp\!\left[
\Lambda\int_0^\eta
-\frac{L(w)H_*(w)}{H_*(w)^2+\sigma^2}\,dw
\right],
\]

with \(L=1-2h\eta^2\), \(H_*=(1/2-h)\eta+(1-\eta^2)(4\eta+0.02)\), and
the declared numerical choices \(h=10^{-6}\), \(\sigma=0.2\),
\(\Lambda=512\), \(C=10^4\).
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{Appendix
B.1, pages 144--145}

The pressure is an analytic test datum with the required evenness and
sign pattern. It has \textbf{not} been obtained by integrating a
prepared outer profile. The parameter values also have not been
certified against the full construction hierarchy. Below we explicitly
identify one joining condition that these test data fail.

That distinction lets us validate the numerical method without
mislabelling the resulting field as the complete paper construction.

\subsection{Regularity turns the equations into a
recurrence}\label{regularity-turns-the-equations-into-a-recurrence}

Expand the normalized swirl, axial velocity, and pressure in powers of
\(Y\). The leading equations contain the radial operators

\[
Y\Phi_{YY}+2\Phi_Y,\qquad YU_{YY}+U_Y.
\]

For a series \(f(Y)=\sum_n f_nY^n\), the coefficient of \(Y^n\) in these
operators is, respectively,

\[
(n+1)(n+2)f_{n+1},\qquad (n+1)^2f_{n+1}.
\]

Neither divisor vanishes for \(n\ge0\). Once the axis values are
supplied, the equations determine the next radial coefficients. Radial
pressure balance supplies the next pressure coefficient through

\[
\Pi_Y=\frac{g^2\Phi^2}{\Lambda}.
\]

The nonlinear right-hand sides also contain axial-parameter derivatives
and radial averages. We compute those using Taylor coefficients in
\(\eta\), retaining enough derivatives for each radial step. This avoids
estimating high derivatives by subtracting nearby floating-point values.
The \href{source-notes.md}{source notes} give the full recurrence and
its derivative accounting. The underlying equations are (4.13) and
(B.15), with the radial inverses described in (B.5).
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
pages 27, 145 and 147}

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Regular normalized swirl and axial velocity profiles are computed from their axis values at four axial parameter locations.}]{figures/01_regular_inner_profiles.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-profiles}Profiles computed with degree 22 and 80
decimal digits at four declared axial parameters. The normalized swirl

\end{figure}%

The smallest sampled \(\Phi\) is about \textbf{0.222}. Sampling
positivity is a numerical observation; it does not certify positivity
throughout a continuous rectangle.

\subsection{Check the equations as the radial degree
increases}\label{check-the-equations-as-the-radial-degree-increases}

The solver reevaluates the leading angular and axial equations and
radial pressure balance from the resulting coefficients. Each relative
defect is the absolute equation imbalance divided by the sum of the
magnitudes of its terms. No fixed numerical floor replaces a small
physical denominator.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Leading balance defects and differences from a higher-degree profile decrease as more radial coefficients are retained.}]{figures/02_radial_degree_convergence.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-convergence}Increasing the radial polynomial degree
reduces both the leading equation defects and the differences from a
degree-24 reference. Degree here means radial Taylor degree of the
leading field; it is not the correction index in the paper's time-scale
expansion.}

\end{figure}%

Across the displayed profile samples, the largest relative angular,
axial, and pressure defects are approximately \textbf{2.6e-22},
\textbf{4.6e-38}, and \textbf{1.7e-16}.

These are leading balances. The source separates axial viscosity and the
remaining radial momentum terms for later correction. Small values here
therefore do not mean the full Navier--Stokes residual has been
cancelled.

We make additional independent checks. Repeating the computation at
higher precision checks retained digits. Independently centered \(\eta\)
expansions agree where they overlap. A separate physical-coordinate test
reconstructs velocity and pressure and uses finite differences to check
incompressibility, the two leading tangential momentum balances, and
centrifugal pressure balance. Its parameters and step size are recorded
explicitly; it is not a global physical-grid simulation.

\subsection{Compare with the source's large-parameter
approximation}\label{compare-with-the-sources-large-parameter-approximation}

Appendix B gives the comparison function

\[
f_0(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-z/2)^n}{n!(n+1)!},
\qquad \Phi\approx f_0(Y\chi),\qquad
\chi=\frac{H_*^2}{H_*^2+\sigma^2}.
\]

The first axial comparison is \(U\approx U_*-YZ_*/(2\Lambda L)\), where
\(Z_*\) is the source's axial expression evaluated at the axis. We test
these formulas at a fixed declared \((Y,\eta)\) while increasing
\(\Lambda\).
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{Equation
(B.13), page 147}

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Computed profiles approach the source\textquotesingle s large-Lambda comparison expressions with the expected observed orders at the selected point.}]{figures/03_large_lambda_comparison.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-asymptotic}At the stated point, the normalized swirl
comparison error decreases approximately as inverse Lambda. After
including the first axial term, the remaining axial difference decreases
approximately as inverse Lambda squared. These are numerical comparisons
for the supplied data, not certified uniform bounds for the
construction.}

\end{figure}%

The experiment tests the algebra and limiting behavior. It does not
determine the theorem's parameter thresholds or prove a uniform estimate
over all axial parameters.

\subsection{Why this does not finish the joined
vortex}\label{why-this-does-not-finish-the-joined-vortex}

The source requires more than local balance. Its axis-data selection
includes condition (B.2): \(\chi>0.99\) wherever the axial expression
\(Z_*\) is sufficiently small. Our test datum has a zero of \(Z_*\) near
\(\eta=\) \textbf{-0.000145}, where \(\chi\approx\) \textbf{0.0093}. It
fails that condition.

This is a concrete reason to keep the example's status explicit. A very
accurate local solution can still have unsuitable data for the later
joining argument.

There is also a representation issue. Some pressure increments are much
smaller than the nonzero axis pressure. Adding them into one pressure
value can lose the increment even at the selected precision. The code
retains those increments separately and differentiates their
coefficients; the
\href{../../code/d6_inner_profile/data/pressure_storage.csv}{pressure-storage
data} expose the loss that occurs if they are recovered by subtraction.

To complete D6, we still need the prepared outer pressure trace,
source-consistent parameter choices, the inner-to-annular continuation
and moment match, and the first correction in powers of \(q^{2h}\).
Increasing radial Taylor degree does not perform any of those steps.

The
\href{../d6_moment_correction/implementation-ledger.md}{implementation
ledger} records that dependency chain. This stage supplies a tested
local recurrence. The \href{../d6_axis_pressure/article.html}{following
pressure study} computes the ideal-schedule pressure datum and supplies
its coefficients to the same engine. The
\href{../../code/d6_inner_profile/study.ipynb}{executed notebook},
\href{../../code/d6_inner_profile/parameters.json}{parameters}, and
\href{../../code/d6_inner_profile/data/}{four CSV datasets} reproduce
the present analytic-data validation experiment.
