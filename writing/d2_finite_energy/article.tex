\subtitle{A taller peak, a smaller region, and the geometry that
connects them}
\author{}
\date{2026-09-11}
\begin{document}
\maketitle


\textbf{A velocity can become arbitrarily large while the energy
associated with its shrinking core decreases.} The reason is that
kinetic energy depends on both the speed and the amount of fluid moving
at that speed. If the region of intense motion shrinks sufficiently
quickly, its decreasing volume can outweigh the increasing speed
squared.

This is one of the most useful ideas to understand in the proposed
OpenAI Navier--Stokes construction. The paper describes unbounded
velocity near a finite time while the full flow's kinetic energy remains
uniformly bounded. Its leading core has an even more striking property:
the energy in that narrowing region tends to zero. To see why those
statements can coexist, we need to look at the geometry as carefully as
the velocity.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Theorem 1.1 and Section 2.1}

We will start with an ordinary function that becomes taller and
narrower. Then we will repeat the argument in cylindrical coordinates,
insert the paper's radial and axial scales, and check the result using a
smooth toy vortex. An animation will show the same changing field in
fixed spatial coordinates and in coordinates that shrink with it.

The goal is to explain why an energy bound alone cannot rule out
concentration of velocity. These calculations reproduce that mechanism;
they do not verify the proposed Navier--Stokes solution or establish
that a real fluid reaches its singular limit. The
\href{../d1_core_size/article.html}{first article} examined the separate
question of physical fluid assumptions.

\subsection{Start with a tall, narrow
bump}\label{start-with-a-tall-narrow-bump}

Imagine a rectangular graph with height \(H\) and width \(w\). The area
under the graph is \(Hw\). If we instead square its height before
integrating, the area is \(H^2w\). This second quantity is the useful
analogy for kinetic energy, because energy depends on velocity squared.

Now make the rectangle narrower while setting \(H=w^{-1/2}\). Its height
grows without bound as \(w\) approaches zero, but

\[
H^2w=(w^{-1/2})^2w=1.
\]

The sharp corners are not essential. A smooth Gaussian gives the same
result:

\[
f_\epsilon(x)=\epsilon^{-1/2}
\exp\left(-\frac{x^2}{2\epsilon^2}\right).
\]

Its peak is \(\epsilon^{-1/2}\). To integrate its square, substitute
\(X=x/\epsilon\), so that \(dx=\epsilon\,dX\):

\[
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_\epsilon(x)^2\,dx
=\epsilon^{-1}\epsilon\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-X^2}\,dX
=\sqrt{\pi}.
\]

The height keeps growing while the squared integral stays fixed. More
generally, replacing \(\epsilon^{-1/2}\) by \(\epsilon^{-p}\) gives

\[
\int f_\epsilon^2\,dx=\sqrt{\pi}\,\epsilon^{1-2p}.
\]

For positive \(p<1/2\) the integral tends to zero; at \(p=1/2\) it stays
constant; for \(p>1/2\) it diverges. In all three cases the peak
diverges. \textbf{The outcome depends on how growth and concentration
compete.}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Gaussian curves become taller and narrower; three amplitude exponents give decreasing, constant, and increasing squared integrals.}]{figures/01_tall_and_narrow.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-bump}The first two panels show the smooth bump and

\end{figure}%

\subsection{A vortex has two widths that
matter}\label{a-vortex-has-two-widths-that-matter}

For a constant-density fluid, kinetic energy is

\[
E=\frac{\rho}{2}\int |\mathbf u|^2\,dV.
\]

The maximum speed asks how large the velocity becomes at its most
intense location. Energy adds the squared speed throughout a region.
These are different measurements. In mathematical notation, they
correspond to the \(L^\infty\) norm and, up to the density factor, the
square of the \(L^2\) norm.

For a core around a vortex axis, a radial width alone is insufficient.
We also need its extent along the axis. Call the two scales \(a\) and
\(b\). In cylindrical coordinates,

\[
dV=r\,dr\,d\theta\,dz.
\]

Set \(r=aR\) and \(z=bZ\). Then

\[
dV=a^2b\,R\,dR\,d\theta\,dZ.
\]

The radial scale appears \textbf{twice}: once from \(dr\) and once from
the factor \(r\). The axial scale appears once. For a velocity of the
form \(U\,\mathbf F(R,\theta,Z)\) with a fixed square-integrable shape,

\[
E=\frac{\rho}{2}U^2a^2b
\int |\mathbf F|^2 R\,dR\,d\theta\,dZ.
\]

The last integral is a fixed shape factor. Thus the useful estimate is

\[
\boxed{E_{\rm core}\ \text{scales like}\ U^2a^2b.}
\]

Using \(a^3\) would assume equal radial and axial scaling. Using only
\(a^2\) would omit the shrinking axial extent. Either shortcut can
change the conclusion.

\subsection{Put in the paper's scales}\label{put-in-the-papers-scales}

The paper writes \(\tau=1-t\) for time remaining in its dimensionless
variables. Section 2.1 gives

\[
a\asymp\tau^{1/2},\qquad
b\asymp\tau^{1/2-h},\qquad
U\asymp\tau^{-1/2-h}.
\]

Here \(U\) represents the dominant azimuthal or axial speed scale. The
radial velocity has a weaker stated scale, \(O(\tau^{-1/2})\). The
parameter \(h\) is small and positive; the introduction gives
\(h<0.01\), and the detailed construction imposes stronger restrictions.
The comparison symbol \(\asymp\) leaves fixed multiplicative constants
unspecified.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Section 2.1, page 4}

Multiplying the two radial factors and the axial factor gives

\[
V_{\rm core}\asymp a^2b\asymp\tau^{3/2-h}.
\]

Multiplying by the squared speed then gives

\[
\boxed{E_{\rm core}\asymp
\tau^{-1-2h}\tau^{3/2-h}
=\tau^{1/2-3h}.}
\]

Because \(h<0.01\), the exponent \(1/2-3h\) is positive. The energy
scale therefore tends to zero as \(\tau\) tends to zero. The radial
component contributes at most the scale \(\tau^{1/2-h}\), which also
tends to zero. This explains the core-energy statement on page 4.

For a numerical example, use the limit \(h\to0^+\) and compare with a
reference stage \(\tau_0\). If the remaining time falls to one millionth
of its reference value, the model gives:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The characteristic speed is \textbf{1,000 times larger}.
\item
  Each characteristic width is \textbf{1,000 times smaller}.
\item
  The characteristic volume is \textbf{one billionth} of its reference
  value.
\item
  The core-energy scale is \textbf{one thousandth} of its reference
  value.
\end{itemize}

The energy multiplication is easy to check:
\(1000^2\times10^{-9}=10^{-3}\).

\subsection{Does a shrinking core violate
incompressibility?}\label{does-a-shrinking-core-violate-incompressibility}

Incompressibility means that a parcel made of the \textbf{same fluid
particles} preserves its volume. If that parcel is squeezed radially, it
must extend in another direction. A region selected because the flow is
particularly intense is a different object: particles can enter and
leave it.

A simple example makes the distinction concrete. Consider the separate
affine velocity field

\[
\mathbf v(x,y,z,t)=
\left(-\frac{x}{2\tau},-\frac{y}{2\tau},\frac{z}{\tau}\right),
\qquad \tau=1-t.
\]

Its divergence is \(-1/(2\tau)-1/(2\tau)+1/\tau=0\). Starting at
\(\tau_0=1\), the exact trajectories are

\[
x=x_0\sqrt{\tau},\qquad
y=y_0\sqrt{\tau},\qquad
z=\frac{z_0}{\tau}.
\]

The material volume factor is \(\sqrt{\tau}\sqrt{\tau}/\tau=1\). Radial
lengths shrink and axial lengths grow.

By comparison, the paper's intense-flow core has both \(a\to0\) and
\(b\to0\), with the radius shrinking faster when \(h>0\). That region is
not a material container. In the paper's central flow, radial inflow and
axial outflow are compatible with a shrinking region of intense motion.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Section 2.1 and Figure 1}

The affine example is used only for this geometric point. It grows with
distance and has infinite whole-space energy; it is not our
finite-energy vortex model.

\subsection{Watch the concentration in two coordinate
systems}\label{watch-the-concentration-in-two-coordinate-systems}

For the animation we prescribe a smooth, purely azimuthal toy velocity,

\[
u_\theta(r,z,\tau)=U(\tau)\,R
\exp\left(\frac{1-R^2-Z^2}{2}\right),
\qquad R=\frac r{a(\tau)},\quad Z=\frac z{b(\tau)}.
\]

Its maximum magnitude is \(U\), at \(R=1\), \(Z=0\), and it vanishes
smoothly on the axis. We set the other two velocity components to zero.
This field is divergence free and has finite energy at each positive
\(\tau\). Its time dependence is prescribed; it is not an evaluated
profile from the paper and does not reproduce the paper's radial inflow
or axial outflow.

\textbf{\href{animations/core_collapse.html}{Open the animation with
play, pause, and a frame slider}.}

The left panel holds spatial coordinates fixed. The high-speed region
becomes smaller while the peak value rises. The right panel divides
positions by the current radial and axial widths, and speed by the
current peak. In those variables, the shape stays fixed.

The color scales deliberately measure different quantities: the left
uses speed relative to the initial peak on a fixed logarithmic scale;
the right uses speed relative to the current peak on a fixed linear
scale. Playback takes equal steps in the logarithm of time remaining, so
movie seconds are not physical seconds. The axes are stretched to fit
each panel; their labeled units specify the geometry.

visible change over a finite range can be extremely small. A
dramatically lengthening-looking schematic would need explicit

\subsection{What the calculation
verifies}\label{what-the-calculation-verifies}

The Gaussian vortex has an exact whole-space energy:

\[
E_{\rm toy}=\frac{\rho}{2}e\pi^{3/2}U^2a^2b.
\]

We independently integrate the same field in Cartesian and cylindrical
coordinates. Both agree with the analytic expression. A quadrature
refinement study separates integration error from the small omitted
Gaussian tail. The
\href{../../code/d2_finite_energy/study.ipynb}{notebook} records the
comparison and lets readers change the scales.

This exact result concerns the \textbf{entire Gaussian toy field}. In
applying its scaling argument to the paper, we estimate the
\textbf{leading core contribution}. The paper's full velocity also
includes an exterior, transitions, and corrections. Showing that the
leading core energy decreases does not by itself bound those other
contributions or prove Theorem 1.1.

What we needed was the speed scale and all three spatial scale factors.
What makes the mechanism work is concentration: the volume carrying the
largest speeds decreases fast enough to outweigh the growth of speed
squared. The computation verifies that balance for explicit smooth
examples and reproduces the paper's stated core-energy exponent.

The next task asks a harder question: what acceleration, pressure,
viscosity, and forcing are required to produce a prescribed flow? That
is where the momentum residual enters. The \href{source-notes.md}{source
and model notes} document the assumptions behind this installment.
