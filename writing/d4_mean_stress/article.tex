\subtitle{From sine waves to momentum transport, with a reproducible
experiment}
\author{}
\date{2026-09-11}
\begin{document}
\maketitle


\textbf{A velocity fluctuation can average to zero and still carry
momentum. If that transport varies from place to place, it contributes a
force to the mean flow.} The reason is that momentum transport depends
on products of velocities: reversing both factors leaves their product
unchanged.

This answers a question left by the
\href{../d3_residual/article.html}{previous article}. There we
calculated the force needed to sustain a prescribed vortex. Some terms
balanced, while others left a residual. Could additional fluid motion
supply part of the missing transport even if its average velocity
vanished?

That is the role of the oscillatory corrections in the proposed OpenAI
construction. Its pulse families supply averaged radial fluxes of
angular and axial momentum.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Section 2.2 and Proposition 7.5}

We will make the underlying mechanism visible in three steps. First,
average a sine wave and its square. Then construct an incompressible
velocity with a chosen average momentum flux. Finally, let its amplitude
vary across space and calculate the resulting mean force, including the
part that pressure can cancel. All examples use a dimensionless periodic
square, so the algebra and the numerical checks can be inspected without
a Navier--Stokes solver.

\subsection{Opposite motions need not cancel their
transport}\label{opposite-motions-need-not-cancel-their-transport}

Imagine a fluid parcel moving upward while carrying more horizontal
momentum than its surroundings. Another parcel moves downward while
carrying less. The mean vertical velocity can be zero, but both motions
contribute to the same net upward transport of horizontal momentum: in
the second parcel, both deviations have the opposite sign.

For a fluctuation \(\mathbf w=(w_x,w_y)\), that transport is measured by
\(w_xw_y\). The simplest arithmetic example is

\[
\langle\sin\phi\rangle=0,\qquad
\langle\sin^2\phi\rangle=\frac12,
\]

where the brackets mean a uniform average over one complete period.
Cancellation of the first average says nothing about cancellation of the
second.

Collect the velocity products in a matrix:

\[
Q=\langle\mathbf w\otimes\mathbf w\rangle
=\begin{pmatrix}
\langle w_x^2\rangle&\langle w_xw_y\rangle\\
\langle w_yw_x\rangle&\langle w_y^2\rangle
\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Here \(Q\) is the momentum covariance per unit density. Its diagonal
entries measure squared fluctuations; its off-diagonal entries measure
correlated transport. In dimensional variables, \(Q\) has units of
velocity squared. Some engineering conventions call \(-\rho Q\) the
Reynolds stress. We retain the positive covariance definition and write
the force sign explicitly.

\end{figure}%

\subsection{Build a velocity with the stress we
want}\label{build-a-velocity-with-the-stress-we-want}

Choose the target

\[
Q_{\rm target}=
\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Can an incompressible velocity produce it? On \(0\le x,y<2\pi\), take

\[
\mathbf w=
\sqrt3\binom{1}{1}\sin[8(x-y)]
+\binom{1}{-1}\sin[9(x+y)].
\]

Each wave's velocity direction is perpendicular to its wavevector. For
example, the first wave points along \((1,1)\) while its phase changes
along \((1,-1)\). Their dot product is zero, giving
\(\nabla\cdot\mathbf w=0\) exactly.

Average over the full square. Each sine averages to zero, each squared
sine averages to one half, and their cross product averages to zero
because the two Fourier modes are different. Thus

\[
\langle\mathbf w\rangle=0,\qquad
Q=\frac32
\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}
+\frac12
\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}
=Q_{\rm target}.
\]

The factors \(3\) and \(1\) are the covariance eigenvalues. Changing
these nonnegative weights changes the prescribed covariance within this
two-direction family. The amplitudes are their square roots because the
velocity product is quadratic.

This realizes a \textbf{global average}. It does not make the
instantaneous product \(\mathbf w(x,y)\otimes\mathbf w(x,y)\) constant
at every point. Nor does a nonzero constant covariance by itself give a
mean force: its spatial divergence is zero.

\subsection{A force requires a change in momentum
flux}\label{a-force-requires-a-change-in-momentum-flux}

For an incompressible velocity, the nonlinear transport can be written
as

\[
(\mathbf u\cdot\nabla)\mathbf u
=\nabla\cdot(\mathbf u\otimes\mathbf u).
\]

Split the velocity into its mean and fluctuation,
\(\mathbf u=\overline{\mathbf u}+\mathbf w\), with
\(\langle\mathbf w\rangle=0\). For an average that commutes with
differentiation, averaging the momentum equation leaves the additional
transport \(\nabla\cdot Q\). Move it to the right-hand side and the
force on the mean is

\[
\boxed{\mathbf F_{\rm wave}=-\nabla\cdot Q.}
\]

This is a force per unit mass. It expresses a familiar balance: a region
gains momentum when more arrives than leaves. A constant flux has equal
inflow and outflow. A changing flux can accelerate one region while
decelerating another.

To demonstrate that change, we now average over \(x\) \textbf{at fixed
\(y\)}. The resulting covariance remains a function of \(y\). This
differs from the full-square average in the previous example.

\subsection{Give the oscillations a varying
envelope}\label{give-the-oscillations-a-varying-envelope}

Let the amplitude be larger near \(y=0\) and smaller near \(y=\pi\):

\[
a(y)=1+\epsilon\cos y,\qquad \epsilon=0.4.
\]

Simply multiplying a divergence-free wave by \(a(y)\) generally breaks
incompressibility. A streamfunction avoids that problem. Define

\[
\psi(x,y)=\frac{a(y)}k\cos[k(x+my)],
\qquad
\mathbf w=(\partial_y\psi,-\partial_x\psi),
\]

with integers \(k=8\) and \(m=1\). The mixed derivatives cancel in the
divergence. Expanding the velocity gives

\[
w_x=\frac{a'}k\cos\phi-ma\sin\phi,\qquad
w_y=a\sin\phi,\qquad
\phi=k(x+my).
\]

The term \(a'\cos\phi/k\) is the small correction that preserves
incompressibility. It matters even though the main oscillation looks
nearly unchanged.

The average velocity is zero at every \(y\). Averaging its products over
\(x\) gives the exact covariance

\[
Q(y)=\frac{a^2}{2}
\begin{pmatrix}m^2&-m\\-m&1\end{pmatrix}
+\begin{pmatrix}(a')^2/(2k^2)&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
\]

Since this matrix depends only on \(y\), the force uses the derivatives
of its second column:

\[
\mathbf F_{\rm wave}
=-\binom{\partial_yQ_{xy}}{\partial_yQ_{yy}}
=\binom{maa'}{-aa'}.
\]

We have obtained a nonzero mean force from a velocity whose mean
vanishes everywhere in the averaging coordinate.

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Oscillatory velocity produces a varying mean flux and nonzero mean force; pressure removes its vertical gradient component.}]{figures/02_spatial_transport.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-force}The horizontal velocity alternates in sign,
with amplitude varying across height. Averaging over x leaves a
spatially varying momentum flux. Differentiating that flux gives
opposite horizontal and vertical force components in this example. A
pressure contribution cancels the vertical component while the
horizontal component remains. These are static fields on a dimensionless
periodic square; no time evolution or particle trajectories are shown.}

\end{figure}%

\subsection{Pressure can cancel some of the
force}\label{pressure-can-cancel-some-of-the-force}

In incompressible flow, pressure balances gradient contributions. Here
the vertical wave force is

\[
-aa'=-\partial_y(a^2/2).
\]

The mean pressure \(p=-a^2/2\) contributes \(-\nabla p=(0,aa')\) to the
right-hand side. After adding it, the remaining force is

\[
\mathbf F_{\rm remaining}=(maa',0).
\]

This horizontal force varies with \(y\). Its two-dimensional curl is

\[
-m\big[(a')^2+aa''\big],
\]

which is generally nonzero, so a pressure gradient cannot remove it. In
the baseline calculation, its largest sampled magnitude is approximately
\textbf{0.427} in the chosen dimensionless units. Its integral over the
periodic square is zero: this transport redistributes momentum.

More generally, an isotropic covariance \(q(\mathbf x)I\) contributes
\(-\nabla q\) and can be absorbed into pressure. The part that changes
the incompressible mean velocity is the divergence-free part of
\(-\nabla\cdot Q\).

This calculation is a snapshot of momentum transport. Keeping the
prescribed fluctuation field fixed in time would require its own
dynamical balance; we have not constructed that balance or evolved a
mean flow.

\subsection{Make sure the grid captures the
oscillations}\label{make-sure-the-grid-captures-the-oscillations}

Uniform periodic samples integrate these resolved trigonometric products
to floating-point accuracy. Spatial derivatives are evaluated with
Fourier differentiation. The baseline uses \(128^2\) samples, excludes
the duplicated endpoint, and resolves both the carriers and their
quadratic products.

The force is checked in two ways: differentiate the averaged covariance,
and separately calculate \((\mathbf w\cdot\nabla)\mathbf w\) before
averaging. Both agree with the analytic expression.

A deliberately coarse \(16^2\) grid gives approximately \textbf{94.9\%
relative covariance error} in the constant-target example. Its
frequency-eight sine is sampled at its zeros, so that entire
contribution is lost. A small numerical divergence does not rescue a
velocity that was sampled incorrectly.

At \(20^2\) samples the covariance average is already accurate, but the
grid still cannot represent every quadratic Fourier mode. With the
chosen carriers, their highest coordinate frequency is 18, requiring
\(N>36\) for full product resolution. An accurate average alone is
therefore insufficient evidence that the entire nonlinear field is
resolved.

The right panel shows a modelling correction, rather than numerical
error. The maximum additional covariance entry is exactly
\(\epsilon^2/(2k^2)\), so doubling \(k\) divides it by four. We use a
resolved \(256^2\) grid for that frequency scan. In this particular
construction, the correction lies in \(Q_{xx}(y)\) and contributes no
divergence, so the exact mean force is independent of \(k\).

\subsection{What restrictions does a realizable stress
satisfy?}\label{what-restrictions-does-a-realizable-stress-satisfy}

Every covariance is positive semidefinite:

\[
\mathbf c^TQ\mathbf c
=\langle(\mathbf c\cdot\mathbf w)^2\rangle\ge0.
\]

This permits negative off-diagonal entries, as our varying-envelope
example demonstrates. It forbids a negative variance. Our two fixed wave
directions impose a further restriction: their covariances can be
combined only with nonnegative squared amplitudes.

The paper has an additional, specific admissibility condition. Its
target is a two-component flux vector \((T_{r\theta},T_{rz})\), not a
complete covariance matrix. Proposition 7.5 selects positive squared
amplitudes from two pulse covariance directions; Equation (4.26)
constrains the target direction through the background shear. Positive
semidefiniteness alone does not check that condition.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Theorem 4.6(iii) and Proposition 7.5}

Our example establishes the averaging mechanism, exact
incompressibility, and a mean force that survives pressure balance.
Evaluating the paper's actual stress and pulse amplitudes requires its
joined background and pulse dynamics, which this toy does not supply.

The next question is dynamical: where can the oscillations obtain their
energy, and why can viscosity eventually extinguish them? That motivates
the next installment on a wave growing in a sheared flow and then
decaying.
