\subtitle{Separate the formula, the stored digits, and the sensitivity
of the problem}
\author{}
\date{2026-09-11}
\begin{document}
\maketitle


\textbf{Computing correction coefficients accurately is only one part of
a reliable calculation. You must also retain enough digits to apply
them, and understand how uncertain inputs change the answer.} These
three questions can have very different answers.

The \href{../d6_moment_correction/article.html}{previous article}
exposed a concrete example. Two weighted integrals became almost
identical as a parameter decreased. Cancelling their prescribed
discrepancies required increasingly large positive and negative
coefficients. The resulting moment cancellation lost accuracy.

Before interpreting that loss as a failure of a fluid construction, we
should determine where it comes from. Is the equation difficult to
evaluate? Are accurate coefficients being rounded too aggressively? Or
is the underlying problem sensitive even when solved accurately?

We answer those questions for the two-moment axial correction, using the
original formula, an equivalent formulation designed to avoid
subtraction error, and independently integrated high-precision
references. This is a numerical audit of one finite operator. It is not
a dynamical stability test of the Navier--Stokes flow.

\subsection{Two moment weights become almost the
same}\label{two-moment-weights-become-almost-the-same}

The axial correction coefficients satisfy

\[
B\boldsymbol\alpha=-\mathbf d,\qquad
B_{1j}=\int Rb_j(R)\,dR,\quad
B_{2j}=\int R^{1-2\lambda}b_j(R)\,dR.
\]

These are the axial equations from the five-moment repair.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Equation (5.16), pages 51--52}

We keep the two smooth, unit-integral bumps centered at \(R=2,3\), with
halfwidth \(0.12\). The first experiment keeps \(\mathbf d=(1,-0.4)\)
fixed and varies \(\lambda\).

For small positive \(\lambda\),

\[
R^{1-2\lambda}
=R\,e^{-2\lambda\log R}
\approx R-2\lambda R\log R.
\]

The difference between the two moment rows is proportional to
\(\lambda\). If the computer first rounds both rows to ordinary double
precision, that small difference can lose most of its meaningful digits.

The mathematical matrix remains invertible for these positive
parameters. At \(\lambda=10^{-18}\), however, the implementation of its
original form produces identical floating-point rows, and the numerical
solve reports a singular matrix.

\subsection{Evaluate the difference before losing
it}\label{evaluate-the-difference-before-losing-it}

Replace the second equation by its difference from the first, divided by
\(-2\lambda\). Its new weight is

\[
g_\lambda(R)=
\frac{R^{1-2\lambda}-R}{-2\lambda}
=R\frac{\operatorname{expm1}(-2\lambda\log R)}{-2\lambda}.
\]

The function \(\operatorname{expm1}(x)\) evaluates \(e^x-1\) accurately
when \(x\) is small, avoiding the subtraction of an already rounded
value close to one.
\href{https://numpy.org/doc/stable/reference/generated/numpy.expm1.html}{NumPy
documentation}

As \(\lambda\) approaches zero, this weight tends to \(R\log R\). It
stays distinct from the first weight \(R\).

The right-hand side must undergo the same transformation:

\[
\widetilde B\boldsymbol\alpha
=\binom{-d_1}{(d_2-d_1)/(2\lambda)}.
\]

This is the same exact linear system in a different form. Its evaluation
preserves the small difference that the original computation lost.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={The original solve loses coefficient accuracy as lambda decreases, while the equivalent expm1 formulation remains accurate against a high-precision reference.}]{figures/01_equivalent_formulations.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-formulations}The equivalent expm1 formulation
retains accurate coefficients throughout the parameter scan, while the
original float64 system loses digits and eventually becomes singular.
The transformed rows have a moderate condition number. Nonfinite
condition estimates from the original floating-point matrix are omitted
from the plotted curve and retained in the CSV data.}

\end{figure}%

At \(\lambda=10^{-12}\), the original coefficient error is approximately
\textbf{0.00019} relative to the reference. The transformed calculation
reduces it to about \textbf{6.5e-17}. Across the displayed scan, the
largest relative coefficient error from the transformed calculation is
\textbf{4.8e-16}.

The reference uses 70-digit arithmetic and independent adaptive
integration of the original moment weights. A further 90-digit check at
the smallest \(\lambda\) confirms the reference's convergence.

\subsection{Accurate coefficients still need enough stored
digits}\label{accurate-coefficients-still-need-enough-stored-digits}

At \(\lambda=10^{-12}\), the two correction coefficients are of order
\(10^{12}\), while the moments we want to cancel are of order one. Their
contributions nearly cancel.

Double precision can represent each coefficient to a small relative
error while still losing an absolute amount large enough to spoil that
cancellation. For example, a relative error of \(10^{-16}\) in a number
of order \(10^{12}\) corresponds to an absolute error of order
\(10^{-4}\).

That is what the computation shows. Although the transformed
coefficients have nearly full double-precision relative accuracy, their
original moment remainder is about \textbf{0.00031} at this parameter.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Moment cancellation deteriorates when large coefficients are stored in float64, while retaining higher precision preserves substantially smaller remainders.}]{figures/02_precision_and_storage.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-storage}High relative accuracy in stored
coefficients does not guarantee small original moment remainders when
large contributions cancel. Higher-precision coefficients preserve the
cancellation only if their additional digits are retained. The left plot
floors remainders below 1e-60 for display; the underlying values are
exported.}

\end{figure}%

The right panel repeats the original solve at 20, 30, 40, and 50 decimal
digits. Keeping those digits improves the moment remainder. Rounding the
resulting coefficients back to float64 restores approximately the same
error floor.

Thus, for a future assembled correction, higher-precision arithmetic may
need to continue through coefficient storage, profile evaluation, and
residual evaluation. A high-precision solve followed by low-precision
storage can discard the improvement.

\subsection{Better arithmetic does not remove input
sensitivity}\label{better-arithmetic-does-not-remove-input-sensitivity}

The transformed matrix has well-separated rows, but its second
right-hand side contains division by \(\lambda\). Uncertainty in
\(d_2-d_1\) can therefore remain strongly amplified.

To test this separately, choose \(\lambda=10^{-10}\) and start from
\(\mathbf d=(1,1)\). Perturb only the second supplied discrepancy. The
bump supports and positive parameter remain unchanged, and both the
original and perturbed problems are solved with the accurate
formulation.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Measured coefficient changes are about seven billion times larger than the relative input changes in the selected sensitive example.}]{figures/03_input_sensitivity.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-sensitivity}A small relative change in the supplied
discrepancies produces a much larger relative change in the correction
coefficients. The accurate algorithm reveals this response rather than
eliminating it. The dashed line indicates equal relative changes and
serves only as a comparison.}

\end{figure}%

For this declared perturbation direction, the measured ratio of relative
output change to relative input change is about \textbf{7.1e+09}. The
exported data record the actual floating-point input change as well as
the nominal perturbation.

This is sensitivity of an algebraic map. It does not mean a velocity
perturbation grows by that factor under Navier--Stokes evolution. Nor
have these perturbed discrepancy vectors been obtained from admissible
full paper profiles.

\subsection{What should future calculations
report?}\label{what-should-future-calculations-report}

This experiment motivates three separate checks:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Coefficient accuracy:} compare against a reference or a
  converged independent evaluation.
\item
  \textbf{Accuracy after applying the correction:} reevaluate the
  original weighted moments or differential residual using the
  coefficients as actually stored.
\item
  \textbf{Sensitivity to inputs:} perturb the supplied data in a
  declared way while preserving the constraints being tested.
\end{itemize}

The distinction between rounding error and problem conditioning is
standard numerical analysis. The finite-precision and perturbation
framework in
\href{https://epubs.siam.org/doi/book/10.1137/1.9780898718027}{Higham's
\emph{Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms}} provides the
broader context.

Here, seven checks cover algebraic equivalence at a well-resolved
parameter, independent high-precision references, quadrature and
precision refinement, coefficient storage, and controlled input
sensitivity. The \href{source-notes.md}{source notes} specify every
error measure.

\subsection{Research decision at this
stage}\label{research-decision-at-this-stage}

The supported result is a useful numerical-analysis note: one
source-defined moment operator needs careful evaluation and, in severe
cases, more retained precision. The proposed change uses an elementary
row transformation and a standard numerical function. We make no claim
of a new general algorithm or a new result about blowup.

The \href{research-decision.md}{research decision note} records what
further evidence would support a paper. The central missing link is
still a constructed leading profile and its actual correction
discrepancies. That would show whether the sensitive parameter and input
regimes examined here arise in the chosen realization, and whether the
numerical improvements enable a meaningful full residual calculation.
