\subtitle{Follow the velocity, momentum flux, energy, and wavelength of
one exact example}
\author{}
\date{2026-09-11}
\begin{document}
\maketitle


\textbf{Viscosity can dissipate energy at every instant while a velocity
disturbance grows.} Growth is possible when the disturbance receives
energy from a background shear faster than viscosity removes it. As the
shear changes the wave's orientation and wavelength, that balance
changes too.

This is the dynamical question behind the
\href{../d4_mean_stress/article.html}{previous article's oscillating
velocities}. We showed that zero-mean motion can carry momentum. But
prescribing an oscillation does not explain where its energy comes from,
how its stress evolves, or why its lifetime might be limited.

The proposed OpenAI construction uses pulses whose growth draws on the
background shear and whose later decay involves viscosity. Its pulse
amplitude equation retains viscous damping explicitly.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Section 2.2 and Equation (7.22)}

We will study an explicit two-dimensional example called a \emph{Kelvin
shearing wave}. It is simple enough to derive, evaluate, and check
against an independent time integrator. We will track its velocity,
wavelength, momentum covariance, and energy budget together. This is a
reduced illustration of growth and decay; it does not reconstruct the
paper's rotating, localized pulse.

\subsection{Start with a shear that tilts a
pattern}\label{start-with-a-shear-that-tilts-a-pattern}

Take a background velocity

\[
\mathbf U=(Sy,0).
\]

Fluid at larger \(y\) moves faster in the horizontal direction. A stripe
painted across the flow therefore tilts: its upper portion moves farther
than its lower portion.

For a wave with phase

\[
\phi(x,y,t)=k_xx+k_y(t)y,
\]

transport by the background requires

\[
k_y(t)=k_{y0}-Sk_xt.
\]

Indeed, \(\partial_t\phi+Sy\,\partial_x\phi=0\). The phase moves with
the background even though its wavevector changes.

We use \(S=1\), \(k_x=1\), and \(k_{y0}=4\), all dimensionless. At
\(t=4\), \(k_y\) passes through zero. Before that instant the wavevector
gets shorter and the distance between wave crests grows. Afterward its
length increases and the crests crowd together.

The wavelength measured perpendicular to the crests is

\[
\lambda(t)=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{k_x^2+k_y(t)^2}}.
\]

This is a wavelength in the laboratory coordinates, not a fixed
wavelength in a coordinate system that moves with the shear. To assign a
dimensional length, multiply it by the chosen reference length
\(L_{\rm ref}\). No fluid-specific reference length is assigned here.

\subsection{One wave can be solved
exactly}\label{one-wave-can-be-solved-exactly}

Write the perturbation vorticity as

\[
\omega'(x,y,t)=\zeta(t)\cos\phi.
\]

The corresponding incompressible velocity is

\[
\mathbf w(x,y,t)=\mathbf B(t)\sin\phi,\qquad
\mathbf B(t)=\frac{\zeta(t)}{K^2(t)}
\binom{-k_y(t)}{k_x},\qquad K^2=k_x^2+k_y^2.
\]

The velocity points along the wave crests, perpendicular to the
wavevector. This gives \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf w=0\) and also makes its
self-advection \((\mathbf w\cdot\nabla)\mathbf w\) vanish. The
two-dimensional background vorticity is constant, so the perturbation
vorticity obeys

\[
\zeta'=-\nu K^2(t)\zeta.
\]

Its exact solution is

\[
\zeta(t)=\zeta_0e^{-\nu I(t)},
\qquad
I(t)=
(k_x^2+k_{y0}^2)t
-Sk_xk_{y0}t^2
+\frac{S^2k_x^2}{3}t^3.
\]

The integral \(I(t)=\int_0^tK^2(s)\,ds\) records the accumulated effect
of diffusion. The cubic term matters at late times because the shear
makes \(|k_y|\) grow approximately linearly.

This is the two-dimensional specialization of a classical exact
shearing-wave family; our axes are interchanged relative to the
reference's background shear.
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/1101.5507v2}{Singh and Sridhar, Equations
(1)--(7)}

The peak perturbation speed is

\[
U_{\rm wave}(t)=|\mathbf B(t)|=\frac{|\zeta(t)|}{K(t)}.
\]

Vorticity decreases under viscosity, but velocity also depends on the
changing wavevector. While \(K\) decreases, velocity can increase even
though \(\zeta\) decreases.

\subsection{See the growth, stress, and decay
together}\label{see-the-growth-stress-and-decay-together}

Choose viscosity \(\nu=0.005\) and an initial peak wave speed of one. In
this example, the speed reaches \textbf{3.63 times its initial value}
near \(t=\,\)\textbf{3.995}. Its phase-averaged kinetic energy increases
by approximately \textbf{13.2 times}.

The heat-equation reference uses the same initial perturbation and
viscosity, with zero background shear. Its wavevector remains fixed, so
its speed decays exponentially from the beginning. A second reference
keeps the shear and sets viscosity to zero, isolating the geometric
amplification.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Wave speed grows transiently, wavelength first lengthens then shortens, and the energy budget separates shear production from viscous loss.}]{figures/01_wave_lifecycle.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-lifecycle}The sheared viscous wave first grows and
then decays. A wave with no shear decays monotonically; an inviscid
sheared wave grows more. The wavelength first increases and then
decreases. The lower panels track energy production, dissipation, and
the sign-changing mean momentum flux. All quantities concern the
perturbation, with dimensionless units and phase averages.}

\end{figure}%

At \(t=20\), the speed is only \textbf{0.000227 of its initial value}.
There is no singularity: this example has a finite transient peak
followed by decay.

\subsection{The energy budget says where growth comes
from}\label{the-energy-budget-says-where-growth-comes-from}

Average over one wave phase. The perturbation kinetic energy per unit
mass is

\[
E=\frac12\langle|\mathbf w|^2\rangle=\frac{|\mathbf B|^2}{4},
\]

and the momentum covariance is

\[
Q_{xy}=\langle w_xw_y\rangle=\frac{B_xB_y}{2}.
\]

Differentiating the exact solution gives

\[
\boxed{\frac{dE}{dt}=
\underbrace{-S Q_{xy}}_{\text{shear production}}
-\underbrace{2\nu K^2E}_{\text{viscous dissipation}}.}
\]

Initially \(Q_{xy}<0\), so \(-SQ_{xy}>0\): the shear-production term
supplies perturbation energy. Viscosity still removes energy. Growth
occurs when production exceeds that loss.

The same statement in terms of speed is

\[
\frac{d}{dt}\log U_{\rm wave}
=\frac{Sk_xk_y}{K^2}-\nu K^2.
\]

At the speed peak these terms balance. Viscosity moves the peak slightly
before the geometric turning time \(t=4\). After \(k_y\) becomes
negative, the production term also becomes negative. Both shear exchange
and viscosity then decrease the perturbation energy.

This particular wave has a spatially uniform phase-averaged covariance.
Its stress divergence is zero, so it does not provide the spatially
varying mean force demonstrated in D4. The affine background is
unbounded and has no finite whole-space energy. Its shear-production
budget must not be interpreted as a closed, finite fluid reservoir or a
finite-energy blowup construction.

\subsection{What happens to the
stripes?}\label{what-happens-to-the-stripes}

The following snapshots show perturbation vorticity on the same
Cartesian window. The color scale uses the \textbf{initial} vorticity
amplitude in every panel, so declining vorticity remains visible instead
of being normalized away.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={Three fixed-window vorticity snapshots show tilted stripes becoming vertical and then tilting the opposite way, with weakening color amplitude.}]{figures/02_wave_orientation.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-orientation}The wave changes orientation as the
background shear moves the pattern. At t=4 its wavevector is shortest
and its velocity is close to its maximum, although vorticity has already
decreased. By t=12 the wavevector has turned, the stripes are closer
together, and the perturbation is weaker. The panels use equal spatial
scales and one fixed vorticity color scale. They show field snapshots,
not particle trajectories.}

\end{figure}%

These are windows into an unbounded-plane solution. They do not impose
walls, and the laboratory field need not be periodic across the top and
bottom of a fixed window at arbitrary times. Shearing coordinates can
support a different, shear-periodic description, but no boundary-value
solver is used here.

Over the full interval, the normal wavelength ranges from about
\textbf{0.392 to 6.283 reference lengths}. The displayed snapshots use
\(256^2\) samples and have at least \textbf{32 samples per coordinate
wavelength}. These sampling counts concern the displayed fields, not a
claim of resolving every possible later time.

\subsection{Check the dynamics
independently}\label{check-the-dynamics-independently}

The analytic solution generates the article's curves. To test it
independently, we integrate the projected momentum equation for the two
velocity amplitudes:

\[
\mathbf B'
=-SB_y\binom{1}{0}
+\frac{2Sk_xB_y}{K^2}\binom{k_x}{k_y}
-\nu K^2\mathbf B.
\]

The middle term comes from the pressure required to preserve
incompressibility as the wavevector turns. We use classical fourth-order
Runge--Kutta, without projecting away any numerical divergence error.

\begin{figure}

\centering{

\pandocbounded{\includegraphics[keepaspectratio,alt={The RK4 amplitude error decreases at fourth order, and the sheared-wave damping eventually overtakes the fixed-wavevector reference.}]{figures/03_time_accuracy_and_damping.png}}

}

\caption{\label{fig-accuracy}Halving the time step reduces the
independent amplitude error approximately as a fourth-order method
predicts. The second panel compares accumulated damping for a changing
wavevector with damping at the fixed initial wavevector. Early
wavelength growth reduces damping relative to the fixed-wavevector
reference; later wavelength shortening strengthens it.}

\end{figure}%

The plotted histories have spacing \(\Delta t=0.01\). The independent
refinement study uses steps from \(0.4\) down to \(0.025\); at the
finest step, its largest vector-amplitude error is approximately
\textbf{1.3e-08}. This is an error against the analytic solution, not an
estimate from comparing two equally uncertain simulations.

Further checks differentiate the velocity and pressure in physical space
and time, substitute them into the primitive momentum equation, verify
the phase-averaged stress, and integrate the energy budget. The
\href{../../code/d5_shearing_wave/validation.json}{validation record}
and \href{source-notes.md}{source notes} record the numerical
conventions.

\subsection{What carries over to the paper's
pulses?}\label{what-carries-over-to-the-papers-pulses}

The example connects three observable quantities: wave orientation
controls wavelength, momentum covariance controls shear production, and
accumulated squared wavenumber controls viscous attenuation.

The OpenAI pulse construction additionally uses rotating and axial
shear, localized envelopes, two admissible flux families, seeding, and
corrections. Our two-dimensional transient growth is a different
mechanism from that complete construction; its initial wavelength
lengthens before shortening.
\href{https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf}{OpenAI,
Sections 7.2--7.3}

There is no small parameter or source calibration here that turns this
Kelvin wave into the paper's actual pulse. It is a fully specified
example that explains why ``viscosity is dissipative'' does not imply
``every disturbance decays monotonically.''

The \href{../../code/d5_shearing_wave/study.ipynb}{notebook},
\href{../../code/d5_shearing_wave/parameters.json}{parameters}, and
\href{../../code/d5_shearing_wave/data/}{CSV data} support experiments
with different viscosity, shear, and initial orientation. The next
deliverable will examine which corrections in the original construction
can actually be evaluated and what inputs they require.
